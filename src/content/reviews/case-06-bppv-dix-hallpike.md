## Medical notes

- The history is typical for BPPV: brief positional vertigo triggered by turning in bed and head movement.
- Posterior canal BPPV should not be confirmed by history alone. It is diagnosed when Dix-Hallpike provokes vertigo with torsional, upbeating nystagmus.
- Correct Dix-Hallpike technique: upright to supine, head turned 45 degrees toward the tested ear and neck extended about 20 degrees, eyes open, observe latency, direction and duration of nystagmus. If the first side is negative, test the opposite side.
- Typical posterior canal nystagmus has latency, increases then resolves, usually within 60 seconds. Persistent, pure downbeat, non-fatigable or neurologically accompanied nystagmus should trigger concern for a central mimic.
- Treatment is canalith repositioning, especially the Epley maneuver for posterior canal BPPV. Routine vestibular suppressants or "vascular" drugs are not the core treatment and can obscure diagnostic nystagmus.
